PTHA in plain words

A probabilistic tsunami hazard assessment (PTHA) answers: how often should we expect each possible tsunami? The largest tsunamis come from great earthquakes on subduction zones, so the first half of the answer, the part this package builds, is: which earthquakes can each subduction zone produce, and how often? This page explains the ideas behind it, one at a time, with the numbers of the Kermadec-Tonga example.

At a glance

1. Subduction zones

The Earth's surface is made of rigid plates that move a few centimetres a year. Where two of them converge and one is oceanic, the oceanic plate bends down and sinks under the other: a subduction zone. The line on the sea floor where it starts to sink is the trench, often the deepest place in the ocean. The surface between the two plates, sloping down from the trench, is the plate interface: a huge fault.

The shape of the interface is known from earthquake locations and seismic imaging; the USGS collects it in the SLAB models: the depth of the plate on a map grid. Step 1 turns that map into depth lines (details).

2. Unit sources

A computer cannot model every possible rupture shape. PTHA18 cuts the interface into cells of about 50 x 50 km, the unit sources, in rows parallel to the trench (Kermadec-Tonga: 73 columns along the trench, 3 rows down the slope, 219 cells). The tsunami of each cell, slipping 1 m, is computed once; any earthquake is then a combination of cells, and its tsunami the same combination of the cells' tsunamis.

3. Magnitude and seismic moment

The size of an earthquake is its seismic moment: the rigidity of the rock times the area that slipped times the slip. The moment magnitude Mw is a logarithm of it: one unit more is 32 times more moment, two units 1,000 times.

M0 = μ × A × D      Mw = (2/3) (log10 M0 − 9.05) M0 in newton metres; μ = 3 x 1010 Pa, the rigidity; A the rupture area (m2); D the average slip (m). 9.05 is the constant rptha uses.

4. How often: the plates set the budget

The plates converge at a known speed (Bird's 2003 model: about 10 cm/yr on Kermadec-Tonga). Over a long time, the slip on the interface must keep up with that motion, part of it in earthquakes and part as silent sliding. The fraction released by earthquakes is the coupling. So the plates give a moment budget per year, and the earthquakes must add up to it: this is the moment balance.

moment rate (N m / yr) = μ × area × convergence / cos(dip) × coupling Kermadec-Tonga, with a coupling of 0.25 (illustration): 3 x 1010 x 5.43 x 1011 m2 x 0.103 m/yr / cos(15°) x 0.25 = 4.3 x 1020 N m per year, the moment of one Mw 9.0 earthquake every 80 years or so.

5. The logic tree: many possible answers

Three numbers of the moment balance are not known well: the coupling, the b-value and Mw_max. Instead of choosing one value of each, PTHA18 tries all combinations of 20 couplings, 20 b-values, 40 Mw_max and 2 shapes of the Gutenberg-Richter curve: 32,000 branches, each a complete, different answer, each with a weight (its prior probability) from the literature.

One long fault, or several shorter ones?

A long zone may not break from end to end: it may break in pieces, segments, each with its own earthquakes. Nobody knows which is right, so PTHA18 keeps both, half and half: the zone as one fault (the unsegmented model), and the zone as the sum of its segments. Several short faults give more moderate earthquakes and fewer giant ones than one long fault, so the two halves disagree most at the largest magnitudes. This package runs the one-fault model by default; --segmented true adds the segments, placed where the published table of Berryman et al. (2015) puts them, the same segments PTHA18 used (Segmented vs unsegmented).

6. Learning from past earthquakes, and uncertainty

Some branches predict many more earthquakes than have happened. The instrumental catalogue (GCMT, since 1976) says how many earthquakes of Mw 7.15 or more the zone really had: 9 on Kermadec-Tonga in 41 years. A Bayesian update raises the weight of the branches that agree with that record and lowers the others. The weights after the update are the posterior.

The result is not one curve but 32,000 weighted curves. The package reports their weighted mean and their percentiles (2.5, 16, 50, 84, 97.5): the band between the 16th and 84th percentile holds the middle two thirds of the weight. This spread is epistemic uncertainty: what we do not know about the zone, not randomness of the earthquakes.

7. What "from scratch" means here

PTHA18 published its results and many of its inputs, but it prepared some inputs by hand: in particular it edited the depth lines of each zone. This package rebuilds every input from public data with fixed rules (the geometry rules), and runs the same calculations as PTHA18's R code, reproduced in Python. Given PTHA18's own inputs, it gives PTHA18's own numbers (validation); given public data, it gives an independent model of the same zone, and of zones PTHA18 never covered (examples).
